## What it is

Preference tuning adapts a model from comparisons instead of one canonical answer. A dataset usually contains a prompt, a preferred response, and a rejected response. The training signal says which behavior should become more likely.

RLHF is the broader recipe: collect demonstrations or rankings, train a reward model, then optimize the policy model with reinforcement learning while constraining drift from a reference model. DPO is a simpler offline preference method that trains directly on chosen/rejected pairs, avoiding a separate reward model and online RL loop.

## Why it exists / when to reach for it

Many agentic behaviors are not captured by a single gold answer. A reviewer may prefer the response that asks a clarifying question, cites uncertainty, avoids an unsafe tool call, or chooses a better plan. Preference tuning exists for that subjective layer: helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, refusal style, verbosity, tone, and task-specific judgment.

Reach for it when you can reliably rank outputs and when supervised fine-tuning has taught the task shape but not the quality frontier.

## The moving parts

- Prompts sampled from realistic traffic.
- Candidate responses, often generated by several models or checkpoints.
- Preference labels from humans, experts, rubrics, or carefully validated judges.
- Policy model and usually a frozen reference model.
- RLHF path: reward model, value head, PPO or related optimizer, KL control, and online sampling.
- DPO path: chosen/rejected pairs, beta or temperature-like strength parameter, and offline training.
- Safety, regression, and distribution-shift evals.

## How it works

In RLHF, a supervised model first learns to answer in the right format. Reviewers rank model outputs. A reward model learns to score outputs from those rankings. The policy model then generates responses, receives reward scores, and is updated to increase reward while staying close to the reference model.

DPO starts from the same preference-pair idea but optimizes the policy so it assigns higher relative likelihood to the chosen response than the rejected one. The DPO paper showed this can recover the preference objective with a classification-style loss, which makes training easier to run and debug than a full RL loop.

## When to use vs alternatives

Use supervised fine-tuning when there are clear target responses. Use prompting and guardrails when behavior can be controlled at runtime. Use eval-time reranking when you only need to choose among generated candidates. Use RLHF-style training when you need on-policy optimization against a learned or programmatic reward. Use DPO when you have high-quality static preference pairs and want a simpler training loop.

## Failure modes & gotchas

- Preference data encodes the values and blind spots of its labelers.
- Reward models can be gamed, producing fluent but wrong or evasive answers.
- Over-optimization can collapse diversity or make the model overly cautious.
- Judge models need calibration; otherwise you train on another model's quirks.
- Pairs must be comparable. If chosen and rejected differ in many ways, the model may learn the wrong feature.
- Alignment gains can hide regressions in factuality, tool use, math, or long-context behavior.

## Minimal code shape (pseudocode/short snippet you write)

```text
pairs = load_pairs(fields=["prompt", "chosen", "rejected"])
reference = freeze(copy(base_model))

candidate = train_dpo(
  policy=base_model,
  reference=reference,
  pairs=pairs,
  beta=0.1
)

compare_pairwise(candidate, reference, heldout_prompts)
run_safety_regression(candidate)
```
